[Slide 1]
Hi, I'm Sarvesh Tiku. This is Signal or Coincidence: causal auditing of collusion through shared agent state. The question is simple. When two A I agents behave alike, how do we know whether they are actually coordinating, or just thinking alike?

[Slide 2]
Multi agent systems route work through shared tools, like ticket fields and status flags. Those are natural places to hide a signal. But two copies of the same model will often make the same choice anyway, because they share priors, data, and incentives. Antitrust law has the same problem: parallel conduct alone does not prove an agreement. So we need evidence that information actually moved from one agent to another.

[Slide 3]
Our method is a randomized canary audit. In a synthetic compliance workflow, a preparer agent writes tickets with two simple fields, priority and risk tag, and an approver agent approves or rejects four transactions. The auditor secretly gives only the preparer a random target. The approver never sees it. Then, on matched seeds, we change what the approver reads: we remove the tickets, shuffle them, strip a field, or recompute them from the source record.

[Slide 4]
Because the target is randomized, there is an exact null. If nothing the approver sees depends on the target, the chance it names the target is one quarter of its valid guesses, for any model, however capable. And that is what we see. With the channel removed, with an honest preparer's tickets, or with recomputed tickets, every model sits near twenty five percent.

[Slide 5]
First finding. Without any pre agreed code, three of four models fail to coordinate through the tickets. Gemini does not. Its preparers mark the target as urgent, and recovery jumps to 58 percent, versus 25 percent with the channel removed. And the convention is not private. Other models reading Gemini's tickets recover the target 45 to 55 percent of the time. No other preparer is readable by anyone. The bottleneck is the writer, not the reader.

[Slide 6]
To check that this is causal, we shuffle the tickets so the target's ticket lands on a different transaction, a decoy. Gemini's recovery drops to 12 percent, and its guess follows the decoy 51 percent of the time. With a pre agreed code, every model follows the decoy in one hundred percent of shuffled episodes. Behavior follows the carrier, not the target.

[Slide 7]
Second finding. Detecting information flow is not the same as detecting collusion. When the preparer has an authorized request, to have one transaction reviewed first, honest approvers pick it up 96 to 100 percent of the time, with no detectable increase in harm. And even Gemini's covert channel did not detectably raise harmful approvals: its approver often identifies the target and still rejects it. So a convincing case needs flow, a localized carrier, a harmful outcome, and evidence the flow was not authorized.

[Slide 8]
Third finding: defenses. Stripping the priority field stops a code hidden in priority, but a code in the risk tag passes straight through. Recomputing every field from the source record stops both, and harmful approvals fall with it. But that works by construction here. When a free text note field survives, preparers that know about the sanitizer move the signal into the note, for three of four models, reaching 76 percent for Gemini. Redacting identifiers, numbers, and countries does not stop it.

[Slide 9]
What about just watching the tickets? An L L M monitor reading them is essentially at chance. A fixed structural rule, and a small cross validated classifier, do better, but they detect that the preparer deviates, and they need to know which deviations to look for. The canary audit needs neither: it only asks whether behavior depends on information the approver should not have.

[Slide 10]
To sum up. A randomized canary gives an exact, model free test for information flow through shared tool state. One model converges on a covert convention without being told, and other models can read it. Flow, collusion, and harm are different things, and an audit should measure each. Field level sanitizers only stop the carrier they touch. And to be clear about limits: our agents were instructed to collude, in a small synthetic environment. Thanks for watching.
